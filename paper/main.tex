\begin{abstract}
Humidity and temperature change what a low-cost optical particle sensor reports, and many sensor boxes already measure
both. Recent deep-learning calibrators nevertheless read the particle signal alone. We propose EnvFiLM, a dilated
convolutional calibrator in which an encoding of the box's measured humidity and temperature scales and shifts every
layer of the particle pathway within fixed bounds (feature-wise linear modulation). On the SensEURCity benchmark of TESLA
(AAAI 2025), evaluated with the benchmark authors' own data and protocol, EnvFiLM lowers city-averaged RMSE by 43\% to
52\% relative to the published SCARE results and is more accurate than published TESLA in all nine city and size cells.
The architecture itself matters: against the same network given the same channels as ordinary inputs, EnvFiLM is
better on 65.6\% of 195 held-out units (35 of 45 trained folds, sign test $p = 2.5\times10^{-4}$), and switching the
modulation off makes it worse on 81\% of units. The advantage grows against true reference instruments, where EnvFiLM
is better on 83.7\% of 153 units, and it carries over unchanged to an independent dataset from Southampton (UK), with
different sensors, reference and climate, where it is better on 22 of 24 Plantower unit cases.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Low-cost air-quality sensors can be deployed far more densely than regulatory monitoring stations, which makes them
a practical route to street-level pollution maps. Their weakness is accuracy. The raw readings of optical
particle sensors differ from reference instruments by tens of percent, and the difference depends on humidity,
temperature and the particles themselves \cite{karagulian2019,crilley2018,diantonio2018}. A calibration model maps the
low-cost reading to the value a better instrument would report. Statistical and machine-learning calibration is well
established when a sensor is co-located with a reference \cite{zimmerman2018,malings2019,barkjohn2021,concas2021}, and
recent work treats calibration of minute-resolved signals as sequence modelling. TESLA \cite{ahn2025} introduced a
benchmark on the public SensEURCity dataset \cite{vanpoppel2023} together with a Transformer that uses logarithmically
binned attention, and SCARE \cite{yun2025} improved on it with compressed bitwise attention. Both models read only the
particle channel.

That leaves useful information unused. Every SensEURCity box also records its own environmental state,
including the internal humidity and temperature of the optical particle counter used as the benchmark target, and this
state explains part of the error that the particle signal cannot. The question is how a model should use it. The usual
answer is to append humidity and temperature as extra inputs. We argue for a different one: let the state modulate how
the particle signal is read, at every layer of the network. EnvFiLM implements this idea with feature-wise linear
modulation on a dilated convolutional backbone, and the evidence in this paper shows that this choice improves calibration. Against
baselines that receive exactly the same information, EnvFiLM wins on most held-out units of the benchmark, wins by a
wider margin against true reference instruments, and replicates on an independent dataset that played no part in its
design.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item EnvFiLM, a calibrator in which an encoder of the on-board state produces a bounded per-channel scale
and shift for every layer of a dilated convolutional network on the log particle signal
(Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item A new state of the art on the TESLA benchmark, measured on the authors' own preprocessed files, together with
baselines that receive the same information (TESLA, PatchTST and a convolutional network given the same channels), a
capacity-matched control and an ablation that switches the modulation off (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item A unit-rotation protocol in which every unit is held out once, which turns a benchmark with one test unit per
city into 195 test cases and gives the comparison real statistical power (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).
\item A true-reference benchmark on the same units, where the advantage of the architecture is larger, and a
replication on an independent dataset with different sensors, reference and climate (Sections~\ref{sec:ref}
and~\ref{sec:soton}).
\end{enumerate}
All design decisions were made on training or validation units, and the pass criteria of every confirmatory test were
written down before the test was run.

\section{Related Work}

\textit{Calibration of low-cost particle sensors.} Linear and multivariate regressions with humidity terms, random
forests and neural networks are standard for co-located calibration
\cite{zimmerman2018,malings2019,barkjohn2021,karagulian2019,concas2021}. Physically motivated corrections model the
hygroscopic growth of particles at high relative humidity \cite{diantonio2018,crilley2018,petters2007}. Neural
calibrators range from feed-forward networks and gradient boosting on the reading, humidity and temperature
\cite{si2020} to recurrent models over the recent history, such as the hybrid DNN and LSTM of Park et al.
\cite{park2021}. In all of these the environment enters as additional input features. The closest learned approach
to ours is the neural correction of Koziel et al. \cite{koziel2025}, where a network maps temperature, humidity and the
raw reading to a multiplicative and an additive correction of the output. EnvFiLM moves the environment inside the
model: it modulates the internal features of a temporal network at every layer, and our ablation shows that
environmental information delivered only at the output is clearly worse (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\textit{Fine-grained sequence calibration.} TESLA \cite{ahn2025} and SCARE \cite{yun2025} treat calibration of
one-minute signals as sequence regression over a 360-minute window and design attention mechanisms small enough for
microcontrollers. Their baselines include linear models, PatchTST \cite{nie2023}, iTransformer \cite{liu2024} and
Mamba. Simple models have often been found competitive with Transformers on time-series tasks \cite{zeng2023}, yet
dilated convolutional networks \cite{oord2016,bai2018} were not among the baselines of either benchmark paper.

\textit{Conditioning.} Feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM) \cite{perez2018} conditions a network by predicting a
per-channel scale and shift from a side input. It is one of a family of feature-wise transformations
\cite{dumoulin2018} that also includes conditional normalisation \cite{devries2017} and hypernetworks, which generate
the weights themselves \cite{ha2017}. In time-series forecasting, the Temporal Fusion Transformer feeds known
covariates through dedicated encoders and gating \cite{lim2021}, and reversible instance normalisation removes the
level and scale of each window \cite{kim2022}. To our knowledge, these ideas have not been applied to low-cost sensor calibration, where
the side information is unusually rich and physically meaningful. This paper asks whether conditioning the particle
pathway on the sensor box's measured state beats giving the same state as inputs, and shows that it does.

\section{Problem and Benchmarks}
\label{sec:problem}

\textit{TESLA benchmark.} SensEURCity \cite{vanpoppel2023} contains one-minute data from 85 AirSensEUR boxes in
Antwerp, Oslo and Zagreb. TESLA \cite{ahn2025} uses each box's first co-location period at a reference station. The
low-cost input is a Plantower PMS5003 channel, and the target is the box's Alphasense OPC-N3 reading of the same size
fraction (\PMten, \PMtwo{} or \PMone). Units are split in a fixed order per city: the last unit tests, the
second-to-last validates and the rest train. The model sees the previous 360 rows of the input and predicts the target
at the last row, and the metric is RMSE in \textmu g/m$^3$. We use the authors' preprocessed files (row counts
identical to their Table A1) and their unit lists, so our numbers are directly comparable with theirs. The authors
dropped rows wherever a value or a quality flag was missing, so consecutive rows are not always consecutive minutes; as
in the original code, windows are taken over rows. Table~\ref{tab:data} summarises the benchmark.

\textit{On-board state.} Each box also records ambient relative humidity and temperature (SHT31), and the OPC-N3
reports its own internal humidity and temperature. We attach these four channels to the authors' rows by timestamp.
They are measured inside the box during deployment, so EnvFiLM needs no extra instrument. Two of the channels belong to
the OPC-N3, which on this benchmark is the target; on the true-reference benchmark the OPC-N3 is simply a second
on-board sensor, and the Southampton dataset uses only the monitors' and the station's humidity and temperature.

\textit{True-reference benchmark.} To confirm that the conclusions hold when the target is a reference-grade
instrument rather than a second low-cost sensor, we build a variant on the same units and periods in which the target
is the station's reference (FIDAS in Antwerp, the station reference in Oslo). Not all references are minute-resolved,
so all signals are averaged to 10 minutes and the window is 36 steps, the same six hours. Zagreb is excluded because
its reference is not sub-hourly. Unit lists and splits are those of the TESLA benchmark.

\textit{Independent dataset.} Bulot et al. \cite{bulot2023} co-located 8 Plantower PMS5003 and 8 Sensirion SPS30
sensors, housed in four monitors, with a FIDAS 200S reference in Southampton (UK) and published 2-minute medians
\cite{bulot2022data}. Each monitor also logged its own humidity and temperature (SHT35) \cite{bulot2022onboard}. We use
the period for which the monitor records exist (July to December 2020, about 0.87 million 2-minute rows per sensor
type). Each sensor's reading of a size fraction is the input and the FIDAS reading of the same fraction is the target.
Every model receives four state channels: the monitor's humidity and temperature, and the humidity and temperature of
the reference station's weather sensor. The window is 180 steps, again six hours. The sensors, the reference, the
climate and the network all differ from SensEURCity.

\section{EnvFiLM}
\label{sec:method}

\textit{Design rationale.} Humidity and temperature do not add a fixed amount to an optical particle reading. Water
uptake changes particle size and refractive index, so its effect on a light-scattering sensor scales with the particle
signal and depends on the size distribution \cite{diantonio2018,crilley2018,petters2007}. The two sensors of a
SensEURCity box also share a housing and its temperature. A model that receives humidity as one more input has to
discover such multiplicative interactions on its own. Feature-wise linear modulation \cite{perez2018} builds them in:
the side input sets a gain and an offset for every feature channel, so the environment can change how strongly each
learned temporal pattern of the particle signal contributes to the output.

In EnvFiLM (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}) the particle window $x_{t-N+1},\dots,x_t$ is first transformed to
$\ell = \log(1 + x)$,
because concentrations are heavy-tailed and optical sensor responses are closer to multiplicative than additive. Seven
convolution layers with kernel size 3, 32 channels and dilations $1, 2, \dots, 64$ cover the window. The on-board state
window $e \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times 4}$ is standardised with training statistics and clipped to $\pm3$ standard
deviations. A summary vector $z$ concatenates, for each channel, the last value, the window mean, the change over the
last hour, and the window minimum and maximum. An environment encoder (a two-layer MLP with 64 hidden units) maps $z$ to
a scale $\gamma_k$ and a shift $\beta_k$ for every channel of every layer $k$:
\begin{equation}
h_k = \mathrm{GELU}\big( (1 + \tfrac12\tanh \gamma_k) \odot \mathrm{Conv}_k(h_{k-1}) + \tfrac12 \tanh \beta_k \big).
\end{equation}
The last layer of the encoder is initialised at zero, so training starts from an unconditioned network and learns how
much conditioning to apply. The bound keeps every scale in $[0.5, 1.5]$, which makes the model robust when conditions
leave the training range (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). The head is an MLP on the last and mean states of the final layer,
the last value of $\ell$, and $z$. EnvFiLM has 54,785 parameters, and Table~\ref{tab:hyper} lists the configuration.
The output is on the scale of the target (divided by 300, as in the original code), and the loss is the mean squared
error.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\textit{Baselines.} Every model is trained with the same recipe (Adam, learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch 512, 10 epochs,
mean squared error, best epoch on the validation unit), so differences come from the models, not from tuning:
\begin{itemize}
\item TESLA, our PyTorch re-implementation of the official code, and the published TESLA and SCARE numbers;
\item TESLA + env.: TESLA with the four state channels concatenated to the particle value before its token
embedding (equal information);
\item LogTCN: the EnvFiLM convolution stack and head without any environmental input;
\item TCN + env.: LogTCN with the four state channels as additional input channels at every time step (equal
information, early fusion);
\item TCN + env., size-matched: the same with 50 channels (52,949 parameters, matched to EnvFiLM);
\item EnvFiLM, modulation off: EnvFiLM with the FiLM path removed, so the state summary reaches only the head;
\item PatchTST \cite{nie2023} with patches of 16 steps (stride 8), three encoder layers of width 64, four heads,
instance normalisation and the log particle input, run on the particle channel alone and, channel-independently, on
the particle and the four state channels (PatchTST + env., 309,825 parameters). Its sizes were fixed in advance.
\end{itemize}
On the authors' data our TESLA re-implementation reproduces the published numbers closely (for example \PMten{} 14.09
versus 14.58 in Antwerp and 13.53 versus 13.87 in Zagreb), which confirms that the shared recipe treats the attention
models fairly.

\textit{Official split.} Three seeds per model and cell; we report the mean.

\textit{Unit rotation.} The official protocol tests one unit per city, and with an identical seed we measured
run-to-run differences of about 7\% on a single unit (GPU non-determinism), as large as the effects of interest. We
therefore hold out every unit once. Per city and size fraction the unit list is cut into five folds; a fold's units
test, two other units validate and the rest train, using every fourth window for all models alike. The comparison with
the same-input TCN uses per-unit RMSE averaged over two training seeds on the TESLA benchmark and four on the
true-reference benchmark; the comparison with the modulation switched off uses one seed. This gives 195 unit cases on
the TESLA benchmark and 153 on the true-reference benchmark. We report the share of units on which EnvFiLM has the
lower RMSE, the median ratio of per-unit RMSEs, a sign test and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test on log ratios. Because
units of one fold share a trained model, we also give a sign test over folds, each fold contributing the mean log
ratio of its units.

\textit{Development and pre-registration.} Model components were chosen on a development protocol that uses only the
official training units (four folds of held-out training units). Before each confirmatory run we wrote down its pass
criteria. The main criterion for the architecture was the rotation test: better on at least 55\% of units, with a
pooled median ratio below one.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{TESLA benchmark}

Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the official-split results. EnvFiLM is more accurate than published TESLA in all nine cells
(mean ratio 0.54) and than our TESLA re-implementation in all nine. Averaged over cities, its RMSE is 8.04 (\PMten),
2.71 (\PMtwo) and 1.36 (\PMone), against 14.03, 5.67 and 2.51 reported for SCARE and 14.75, 6.16 and 3.18 for TESLA,
which roughly halves the error of the current state of the art. EnvFiLM also leads the baselines that receive the same
information: it is lower than the TCN with the same channels in 6 of 9 cells (mean ratio 0.95), than its size-matched
version in 7 of 9 (0.97) and than TESLA with the same channels in 7 of 9 (0.79). PatchTST with the same channels is the
strongest Transformer we tested, and EnvFiLM beats it in 6 of 9 cells (mean ratio 0.94) and on the city average of
every size fraction (8.04, 2.71 and 1.36 against 8.61, 3.11 and 1.53) with less than a fifth of its parameters. On the
particle channel alone, PatchTST is worse than LogTCN in all nine cells.

The on-board channels account for much of this gain. LogTCN, which does not use them, already improves on published
TESLA in all nine cells, and adding the channels helps further. On the development protocol, ambient humidity alone
lowered the per-unit RMSE of LogTCN on 79.7\% of 118 held-out training units (median ratio 0.91), and the four channels
improved on humidity alone on 79.0\% of 62 (median 0.93). The oracle analysis of Section~\ref{sec:limits} shows why the
state matters: the ratio between the two sensors moves from day to day with an interquartile range of 45\% of its
median, and the daily ratio correlates with humidity and with the concentration level.

\subsection{Is it the architecture or the information?}
\label{sec:ablation}

The unit rotation tests whether the architecture, and not only the extra information, is responsible
(Fig.~\ref{fig:rot}). Over 195 held-out units, with each unit's RMSE averaged over two training seeds, EnvFiLM is better
than the TCN with the same four channels on 65.6\% of units (bootstrap 95\% interval over units 59.0\% to 72.3\%). The
median per-unit ratio is 0.974 (interval 0.960 to 0.984; sign test $p = 1.5\times10^{-5}$; Wilcoxon
$p = 1.6\times10^{-6}$), and the mean RMSE is lower in six of the nine city and size groups (Table~\ref{tab:groups}).
The result does not depend on how the units are counted: over trained folds, EnvFiLM is better in 35 of 45 (sign test
$p = 2.5\times10^{-4}$), and each seed on its own favours EnvFiLM (62.6\% of units with median ratio 0.967 for the
first seed, 59.0\% with 0.984 for the second).

The ablation gives a clearer result. Against the same model with the modulation switched off, EnvFiLM is better on 81.0\% of
units (interval 75.4\% to 86.2\%; median ratio 0.912, interval 0.890 to 0.928; Wilcoxon $p = 6.7\times10^{-17}$; 39 of
45 folds). The environment is much more useful when it changes the particle pathway than when it reaches only the
output head. The TCN given the same channels is a stronger baseline, because it also lets the environment interact with
the particle signal from the first layer on. We attribute EnvFiLM's lead to an interaction that is multiplicative
and conditioned on a summary of the whole window, instead of being learned implicitly from concatenated channels. Model
size does not explain the gain: on the development protocol EnvFiLM was better than the size-matched TCN on 68.8\% of
93 units (median 0.951).

\subsection{True-reference benchmark}
\label{sec:ref}

Against reference-grade targets the advantage of EnvFiLM grows. Table~\ref{tab:ref} reports the official split of the
true-reference benchmark with unchanged models and hyperparameters. (At 10-minute resolution the ``last hour'' change
in $z$ spans the whole window; we left the feature as it was.) EnvFiLM has the lowest error in five of six cells and is
tied in the sixth (\PMtwo{} Oslo, 1.40 versus 1.39), and it is lower than TESLA and than TESLA with the same channels in
all six (mean ratios 0.45 and 0.48). In the rotation, with each unit's RMSE averaged over four training seeds, EnvFiLM
is better than the TCN with the same channels on 83.7\% of 153 units (bootstrap 95\% interval 77.8\% to 89.5\%). The
median ratio is 0.946 (interval 0.939 to 0.957; Wilcoxon $p = 1.8\times10^{-17}$; 26 of 30 folds, sign test
$p = 5.9\times10^{-5}$), and the mean is lower in five of six groups. In the best trainings the margin is wider still:
two of the four seeds are better on 88.9\% and 88.2\% of units, with median ratios of 0.893 and 0.896, and no seed is
worse than the TCN. The advantage is most likely larger and more uniform here than on the TESLA benchmark because all units
share one reference instrument, so the unit-specific gain of the low-cost target sensor, which no input can reveal
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}), no longer dominates the error.

\subsection{Independent dataset}
\label{sec:soton}

Southampton is the strictest test in the paper, because its sensors, reference and climate all differ from
SensEURCity and its data played no part in any design decision. We ran the unit rotation within each sensor type (8 units, five
folds, two validation units, every fourth training window) with models and hyperparameters unchanged from SensEURCity
and per-unit RMSE averaged over two training seeds, under pass criteria written down before the run. EnvFiLM passed
clearly (Table~\ref{tab:soton}). For the PMS5003 sensors it is better than the TCN with the same four channels on 22 of
24 unit cases (median ratio 0.959, Wilcoxon $p = 5.1\times10^{-6}$; 75.0\% and 87.5\% for the two seeds separately) and
better than TESLA with the same channels on all 24 (median 0.859). For the SPS30 sensors it beats TESLA with channels on
22 of 24 (median 0.821) and edges the TCN with channels (14 of 24, median 0.988). Its mean RMSE is the lowest of the
three models in all six sensor and size groups, and over both sensor types it is better than the TCN with channels in
25 of 30 trained folds (sign test $p = 3.2\times10^{-4}$).

EnvFiLM does not depend on a particular source of state information either. Repeating the run on exactly the same rows
and seeds with the station's weather channels (humidity, temperature, dew point and pressure) in place of the monitors'
sensors, EnvFiLM again beats the TCN with channels, on 19 of 24 PMS5003 cases (median 0.990) and 20 of 24 SPS30 cases
(median 0.973), and TESLA with channels on 23 and 22 of 24. The method therefore works both with sensors built into the
monitor and with a nearby weather station.

\subsection{Cost}

EnvFiLM is compact. It has 54,785 parameters, against 23,681 for the TCN with channels, 4,531 for TESLA with channels
and 309,825 for PatchTST with channels. One training run of EnvFiLM took 4 to 17 minutes per cell on our GPU, roughly a
tenth of the 30 minutes to 2.7 hours needed by PatchTST with channels, which is also why PatchTST was not included in
the unit rotation. A single 360-minute window takes 2.9 ms on one CPU core in PyTorch, against 1.9 ms for the TCN with
channels and 1.0 ms for TESLA with channels, well within the budget of server-side calibration. The microcontroller
designs of TESLA and SCARE remain smaller.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textit{Why a convolutional network beats the attention models here.} Our re-implementation of TESLA, trained with the
same recipe as every other model, reproduces the published numbers, so the gap between TESLA and LogTCN is not a
matter of training. Two properties of the benchmark favour the convolutional design. The useful information is local
and recent (the target is the reading at the last minute of the window), which dilated convolutions capture directly
and logarithmic binning of older tokens partly discards. The input is also heavy-tailed, which the log transform
handles and the raw-scale input of TESLA does not. In early exploratory runs on our own reconstruction of the benchmark
from the raw archive, before we used the authors' files, a log-scaled input did not improve TESLA (7.55 versus 7.04 on
Oslo \PMtwo) but did improve the convolutional network (from 5.12 to 4.47), so the difference lies in the architecture
rather than in the scaling alone. This is in line with the broader finding that simpler models are often competitive
with Transformers on time-series tasks \cite{zeng2023}, and with our PatchTST results.

\textit{Why modulation helps more against a true reference.} On the TESLA benchmark each unit is compared with its own
OPC-N3, whose unit-specific gain adds an error that no input can explain (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). Against a shared
reference instrument that error disappears, and a larger part of what remains depends on the sensor's state, which is
what EnvFiLM conditions on. The larger and more uniform advantage on the true-reference benchmark
(Table~\ref{tab:groups}) fits this reading, and it is the setting that matters most in practice, since deployed
networks are calibrated against reference stations.

\textit{Practical use.} The state channels EnvFiLM needs are measured inside every AirSensEUR box and many commercial
monitors, so it needs no extra hardware, and Southampton shows that a nearby weather station works as well. At 2.9 ms
per window on one CPU core it is cheap enough for server-side calibration of a large network.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limits}

\textit{Ceiling of the TESLA benchmark.} Fitting the best scalar gain per held-out training unit, with test labels and
only for diagnosis, removes a median of 19\% of the error variance, and the remaining error is still 4.9 times the
minute-to-minute noise of the target. Across co-located units, the gains of the input sensors relative to the fleet
have an interquartile range of about $\pm3\%$ and those of the OPC-N3 targets about $\pm10$ to 15\%. Part of the error
on this benchmark therefore belongs to the target instrument and cannot be predicted from any input, which also
explains why the true-reference benchmark separates the models more clearly.

\textit{What the modulation responds to.} The design was motivated by the humidity response of optical sensors, and
the model's behaviour turns out to be broader than that explanation. Splitting each official test unit's windows into
humidity terciles, the advantage over the TCN with channels is not confined to the most humid third: it is larger in
the driest third in 6 of 9 cells, and the modulation is most active in drier air in 8 of 9 cells. EnvFiLM is therefore capturing more than the hygroscopic effect, and identifying what else it tracks is a
direction for future work.

\textit{Seasons.} Calibrations trained in one season and applied eight to ten months later degrade for every model:
when we trained on the first co-location (spring 2020) and tested on each unit's second co-location, the errors of the
TCN with channels grew by factors between 1.2 and 5.4 (for example \PMten{} in Antwerp from 6.7 to 26.2), and the
ratio between the two sensors itself shifted by up to $\pm50\%$. EnvFiLM does not solve this either (better than the
TCN with channels on 35.9\% of 128 units, median ratio 1.034), and on Southampton, over the full year with weather
channels and one seed, it was level with the TCN with channels (11 of 24 PMS5003 cases, median 1.002). Periodic
recalibration, which is standard practice, remains necessary.

\textit{Training.} The modulation path starts at zero and does not engage in every run within the ten-epoch budget. On
the true-reference benchmark two of four seeds gave EnvFiLM a clear advantage and two left it level with the TCN, so we
average several trainings, which recovers more of the advantage (83.7\% of units, median ratio 0.946) than selecting
the seed with the lowest validation error (73.2\%, 0.951). On the TESLA benchmark both seeds favoured EnvFiLM. A
longer schedule or a warm start of the modulation is a natural next step.

\textit{Bounded modulation is necessary.} An unbounded version of EnvFiLM (scale $1 + \gamma$, no input clipping)
performed similarly in season, being better than the TCN with channels on 67.7\% of 195 units, but failed badly out
of season, where conditions leave the training range: its median per-unit ratio to the TCN with channels was 11.4
and its worst unit 111. With the bound, the worst unit was 1.26.

\textit{Design exploration.} The final design is not the first we tried. On the development protocol we also tested a
physically shaped humidity gate following the $\kappa$-K\"ohler growth law \cite{petters2007} (better than the TCN
with humidity on 40\% of units, median ratio 1.05); a latent gain for each training unit's target sensor (44\%, 1.01);
residual blocks with dropout, multi-scale input and a log-normal output head (residual blocks better on 3\% of units,
median 1.11); a multiplicative gain head with a label-free context of the unit's own recent readings and gain
augmentation; and self-supervised joint-embedding pretraining of the convolution trunk on 32 million unlabelled
minutes from the whole fleet \cite{assran2023,bardes2022}. None improved on the plain network on validation units,
which suggests that EnvFiLM's gain does not come easily from more elaborate machinery.

\textit{Validity.} Because the official split has one test unit per city and GPU training is not bit-reproducible, we
rest the architectural claim on the unit rotation, which uses two to four seeds for the main comparison and every
fourth training window for all models alike. Model components were chosen on the official training units, which also
appear in the rotation; the true-reference benchmark and the Southampton dataset, which played no part in design,
confirm the result independently. The on-board channels are attached to the authors' rows by timestamp, with gaps
interpolated within each unit. Our TESLA re-implementation follows the official code with identical sizes; SCARE is
cited from its paper. The Southampton dataset is a single site with 16 sensors in four monitors over half a year, and
broader multi-site replications would strengthen the case further.

\section{Conclusion}

Low-cost particle sensor boxes already measure humidity and temperature, and this state is worth using well. EnvFiLM,
a dilated convolutional calibrator with bounded feature-wise modulation, roughly halves the error of the published
state of the art on the TESLA benchmark. It beats the same network given the state as ordinary inputs on most held-out
units, by a wider margin when the target is a true reference instrument, and it carries over unchanged to an
independent dataset with different sensors, reference and climate. More generally, our results suggest that when a
sensor reports its own operating conditions, they are most useful as a way of adjusting how its signal is read.
